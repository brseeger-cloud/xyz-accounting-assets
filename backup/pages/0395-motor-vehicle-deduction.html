Motor vehicle deductions for sole traders

A sole trader claiming a car has two methods. Cents per kilometre pays 88c a business kilometre for 2025-26 and 91c for 2026-27, capped at 5,000 km per car. The logbook method claims your business-use share of the real running costs. Heavier vehicles such as one-tonne utes and vans are claimed on actual costs instead.

Updated 16 September 2026 Current for 2025-26 and 2026-27

First: is it a car?

For tax purposes a car is a motor vehicle designed to carry a load of less than one tonne and fewer than nine passengers. Sedans, SUVs, station wagons and most dual-cab utes are cars. Only cars can use the cents per kilometre or logbook methods.

Utes and vans rated to carry one tonne or more, vehicles seating nine or more, and motorcycles are not cars. For those you claim the business-use percentage of actual costs, supported by records that show how you worked out that percentage.

Which trips count

Business travel includes driving to clients and job sites, between two places of work, to pick up supplies, and to the bank or post office for the business. Carrying bulky tools you need at the site, with nowhere secure to leave them, can also count.

Driving from home to your regular place of business and back is generally private travel, even for a sole trader. The exception is where your home genuinely is your base of operations and you travel out from it to jobs.

Method 1: cents per kilometre

Income year Rate Maximum claim per car
2025-26 88c per km $4,400
2026-27 91c per km $4,550

Multiply your business kilometres, up to 5,000 per car, by the rate. The rate already includes fuel, registration, insurance, servicing and depreciation, so you can’t claim any of those on top.

You don’t need receipts, but you do need a reasonable basis for the kilometres, such as a diary of regular trips or an app log. “About 5,000” with nothing behind it is the claim the ATO most often reduces.

Method 2: logbook

Keep a logbook for 12 continuous weeks that represent your normal business use. Record the start and finish odometer readings, date, distance and purpose of every business trip. Your business-use percentage is business kilometres divided by total kilometres for those 12 weeks.

You then claim that percentage of your actual car costs for the year:

  • Fuel and oil
  • Registration and insurance
  • Repairs, servicing and tyres
  • Interest on a car loan, or lease payments
  • Decline in value (depreciation)

A valid logbook can support your claims for up to five years, as long as your pattern of use hasn’t changed. Record your odometer at the start and end of every income year, and keep receipts for everything except fuel and oil, which can be estimated from odometer readings.

Which method comes out ahead

The logbook usually wins when business use is high or the car is expensive to run. Take a car costing $12,000 a year to run, used 70% for business. The logbook claim is $8,400. Cents per kilometre can never exceed $4,400 for 2025-26.

Cents per kilometre suits lower mileage, cheaper cars and anyone who won’t keep a logbook going for 12 weeks. You can choose a different method each year, which is a good reason to start a logbook now even if you don’t need it yet.

Buying a car for the business

If you use the logbook method, you can claim the business portion of the car’s decline in value, subject to the car limit. Small businesses using simplified depreciation may be able to write off the business portion faster, depending on the instant asset write-off threshold that applies in the year you buy. Check the threshold for that year before you sign. It has changed repeatedly, and timing the purchase around 30 June can move the deduction a full year.

If the car is owned by your company or trust rather than by you as a sole trader, different rules apply, including fringe benefits tax. That’s a structure conversation, not a choice of claim method.

Work out where it lands

Your vehicle claim reduces your business profit, and with it your tax and any PAYG instalments. The sole trader tax calculator shows the effect, and tax deductions for sole traders covers the rest of what you can claim.
