What can I claim on tax?

You can claim an expense if you spent the money yourself, it directly relates to earning your income, you were not reimbursed, and you have a record of it. Those four tests decide every claim.

Updated 21 August 2026 Current for 2025-26, 2026-27

Every deduction argument comes back to the same four tests. Get them right and the rest is detail.

The four tests

  1. You spent the money yourself and were not reimbursed by your employer.
  2. The expense directly relates to earning your income — not to getting a job in the first place, and not to your private life.
  3. You have a record. Receipts, invoices, a logbook, a diary.
  4. If it is part private, you apportion it to the work-use share, honestly.

An expense that fails any one of these is not deductible, however reasonable it feels.

What most people can claim

Working from home. The fixed rate covers electricity, gas, internet, phone and stationery in one figure per hour worked at home. You need a record of the actual hours across the whole year — an estimate or a four-week sample is no longer accepted. This is the single largest claim for most office workers.

Phone and internet. If you claim the fixed rate for home, they are already in it. If you do not, work out your work-use percentage from a representative four-week period and apply it to the bill.

Tools and equipment. Anything under $300 is deductible immediately. Above that you claim the decline in value over its effective life. A laptop used 60% for work is 60% deductible either way.

Uniforms and protective clothing. Compulsory uniforms with a logo, occupation- specific clothing like chef’s checks, and genuine protective gear. Laundry at

$1 per load for work-only clothing, or $0.50 where it is mixed in with personal washing.

Self-education. Deductible where the study maintains or improves the skills you use in your current job. Not deductible where it qualifies you for a new one — that is the line the ATO litigates most often.

Car expenses. Between workplaces, to see clients, or to a second job on the same day. Claim 88c per kilometre up to 5,000 km without a logbook, or actual costs with one. The trip from home to your regular workplace is private, and the “I carry bulky tools” exception is much narrower than tradies think.

Professional costs. Union fees, professional association memberships, registration and licensing, industry journals and subscriptions.

Income protection insurance. The premium is deductible where the policy covers loss of income. Life, trauma and TPD cover is not.

Last year’s accountant fee. The cost of managing your tax affairs is deductible in the year you pay it, including the fee for the return that claimed it.

What you cannot claim

  • Travel between home and your regular workplace, whatever the hour
  • Meals on a normal working day, including on night shift
  • Plain clothing, even if your employer specifies a colour
  • Childcare, however necessary it is to your working
  • Anything your employer reimbursed you for
  • Fines, including parking fines incurred on the job
  • Entertainment, however business-related the conversation

What a deduction is actually worth

A deduction is not a refund. It reduces the income you are taxed on, so a

$1,000 claim at the 30% bracket is worth $320 once the Medicare levy is counted — not $1,000.

This is worth internalising, because it settles the “should I buy it before 30 June” question. Buying a $2,000 laptop you do not need to save $640 of tax leaves you

$1,360 down.

From 2026-27: the $1,000 floor

For the 2026-27 year onwards, the $1,000 standard deduction gives you a floor with no receipts required. You take the higher of that or your itemised claim. If your real expenses come to more than $1,000 — and for most people with a home office, a uniform or professional registration they do — keep the records and claim the real figure.
