The part that catches people

If no beneficiary is presently entitled to the trust’s income as at 30 June, the trustee is assessed on it instead — and that may be at the top rate of tax. It is a signature on a date, and missing it is the most expensive clerical error in the structure.

The other one is distributions that exist only on paper. Where income is allocated to an adult child or a related entity but the money goes somewhere else, the ATO looks at it under the reimbursement agreement rules in section 100A. We would rather set the distribution up so the question never arises.

Unit trusts, when the owners need fixed shares

A discretionary trust is the standard choice for a family. Where the people involved are not family — two business partners, or two households buying a property together — a unit trust usually fits better. Each owner holds units, and income and capital follow the units, much like shares in a company. The trustee has no discretion over who receives what.

Setting one up follows the same steps: a deed, a trustee (often a company, so no individual holds the assets personally), the first issue of units recorded in a unit register, then the TFN, ABN and GST registrations. We also recommend a unitholders’ agreement covering what happens when someone wants out.

Two things to know before you start. Some states charge duty on the deed or on later transfers of units, particularly where the trust holds land, and the land tax treatment differs from state to state. We give you the figures for your state, alongside our fixed fee, before anything is signed.
